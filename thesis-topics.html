MSc thesis · machine learning

Machine learning for robust and interpretable physiological monitoring in anaesthesia

Modern anaesthesia generates large volumes of high-resolution physiological data, ECG, arterial blood pressure, oxygen saturation, respiratory signals, anaesthetic depth indices, drug administration records, and perioperative outcomes. Open datasets such as VitalDB provide an unprecedented opportunity to develop ML methods for intraoperative monitoring, early warning systems, and clinical decision support. Several research directions are available depending on student interests.

→ View full description on Canvas
Objectives & alternative topics

Alternative 1, predicting anaesthetic depth from physiological signals

Develop models that estimate anaesthetic depth or BIS-related states from multimodal physiological recordings. The work will compare classical signal processing, interpretable ML, and deep learning to better understand the physiological determinants of anaesthetic depth.

Alternative 2, personalised anaesthesia monitoring

Investigate whether patient-specific and adaptive ML models can improve prediction of physiological deterioration during surgery, accounting for inter-patient variability in physiological responses to anaesthesia, surgical stimulation, and pharmacological interventions.

Alternative 3, early prediction of intraoperative hypotension

Develop ML models capable of predicting hypotensive episodes several minutes before onset using multimodal physiological waveforms, vital signs, drug administration data, and patient characteristics, providing clinically useful early warning systems.

Alternative 4, data quality assessment and artefact detection

Create automated methods for identifying artefacts, sensor failures, calibration problems, missing-data patterns, and unreliable waveform segments in large-scale physiological datasets, improving both research reproducibility and clinical AI reliability.

Methodology

The project will primarily use the VitalDB dataset, which contains thousands of surgical cases with synchronised physiological waveforms, numerical monitoring variables, laboratory measurements, medication administration records, and perioperative clinical information.

Depending on the selected direction, the student will preprocess high-resolution signals (ECG, arterial blood pressure, plethysmography, capnography, SpO₂, anaesthesia monitoring parameters), define clinically relevant prediction tasks, and develop appropriate ML solutions.

Potential methods include signal processing, feature engineering, gradient boosting, temporal convolutional networks, RNNs, transformers, representation learning, anomaly detection, and explainable AI. Particular emphasis will be placed on model robustness, interpretability, patient-level validation, uncertainty estimation, and clinically meaningful evaluation.

Expected outputs

A reproducible ML pipeline for analysing large-scale physiological monitoring data, together with a quantitative evaluation of predictive or diagnostic models for a clinically relevant perioperative task. Expected deliverables include a well-documented codebase, benchmark comparisons of modelling approaches, analysis of clinically relevant physiological features, and recommendations for reliable AI-based monitoring. Results may be suitable for conference or journal publication.

MSc thesis · machine learning

Beyond affect labels: evaluating and improving emotion annotation strategies for physiological computing

Affective computing systems rely on labelled physiological datasets to train emotion recognition models. However, the quality of the annotation process itself often has a larger impact on model performance than the choice of algorithm. Existing datasets use a wide variety of labelling approaches (protocol-based labels, continuous self-reporting, post-hoc questionnaires, ecological momentary assessments) yet little work has systematically investigated which strategies produce labels most consistent with the underlying physiological signals.

→ View full description on Canvas
Objective

Extend and improve a recently proposed affect-label validation framework for physiological datasets. The primary objective is to compare different annotation methodologies and determine which types of labels exhibit the strongest correspondence with physiological signals.

As a secondary objective, the student may investigate novel validation metrics and alternative labelling strategies, including personalised labels, consensus-based annotations, temporal smoothing, uncertainty-aware labelling, ordinal labels, and different discretisation strategies. The work may also explore whether certain annotation schemes systematically overestimate the emotional information contained in physiological signals.

Methodology & datasets

The project will analyse multiple affective computing datasets collected using different annotation methodologies and emotional elicitation protocols. Datasets in italics are already part of prior work:

  • WESAD, protocol-based labels assigned by experimental design
  • CASE, continuous real-time valence/arousal annotations
  • DREAMER, post-hoc SAM questionnaires
  • DEAP, valence, arousal, dominance and liking ratings
  • MAHNOB-HCI, multimodal recordings with self-assessment labels
  • SWELL-KW, stress and user modelling dataset

The work will include: (1) implementation and extension of the existing validation framework, (2) development of additional label-quality metrics, (3) comparison of annotation schemes within and across datasets, (4) analysis of temporal alignment between labels and physiological signals, and (5) evidence-based recommendations for affective dataset design.

Expected outputs

An extended framework for evaluating affective label quality, a systematic comparison of annotation methodologies, and practical recommendations for researchers collecting new affective datasets. Deliverables include a reproducible benchmarking framework and a well-documented software pipeline. Results may be suitable for conference or journal publication.

MSc thesis · computer vision · biomechanics External collaboration

Markerless 3D motion capture

3D motion capture is a common technique in biomechanical research and is at the forefront of clinical motion research, rehabilitation, and improving the performance of world-class athletes. However, most techniques require dedicated environments with specialised software and hardware (such as VICON, which uses infrared cameras and reflective markers placed on the participant's body), together with biomechanical models such as the Conventional Gait Model 2 (CGM2). Markerless tracking technology is now developing rapidly and is increasingly applied in biomechanical research. The Department of Nutrition and Movement Sciences is setting up and evaluating a markerless tracking system running on open-source software and recently acquired AI-supported cameras.

Objective

Implement and improve a markerless 3D motion capture system based on open-source software and AI-supported cameras, and evaluate its performance against the current gold standard for marker-based motion capture.

Methodology

The project builds on the markerless tracking system being set up at the Department of Nutrition and Movement Sciences, which combines open-source markerless tracking technology (e.g. OpenPose) with recently acquired AI-supported cameras.

The student will work on the implementation and improvement of this system and compare its output with the gold standard: marker-based motion capture using VICON infrared cameras and reflective markers, combined with the Conventional Gait Model 2.

BSc thesis · computer science students only

Design and evaluation of reliable conversational assistants for university courses

University students frequently need information about course organisation, assessment procedures, deadlines, and learning materials. Although this information is available in official course documents, finding relevant details can be time-consuming. Conversational assistants based on large language models (LLMs) offer a natural way to access this information, but may provide inaccurate, unsupported, or misleading answers. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) combines document retrieval with language generation to address some of these limitations. This thesis focuses on developing and evaluating a safe, user-friendly, server-based chatbot that answers questions using a predefined collection of course documents. Several research directions are available, focusing on information retrieval, answer reliability, and user trust. These directions can be explored individually or combined, depending on student interests and the agreed project scope.

Objectives & alternative topics

Alternative 1, improving document retrieval and answer accuracy

Investigate how different information retrieval strategies influence the accuracy and relevance of answers generated from course documents. The work will compare approaches such as keyword-based search, semantic retrieval using embeddings, and hybrid retrieval. Different document segmentation and ranking strategies may also be explored. The objective is to identify which approaches provide the most accurate and efficient retrieval of course-specific information, particularly for ambiguous or complex questions.

Alternative 2, preventing hallucinations and improving answer reliability

Investigate mechanisms that prevent a conversational assistant from generating incorrect, unsupported, or misleading answers. The work will explore evidence-based answer validation, confidence estimation, and strategies for recognising questions that cannot be answered using the available documents. Particular attention will be paid to the trade-off between answering useful questions and refusing those for which sufficient evidence is unavailable. The objective is to develop and evaluate mechanisms that improve reliability without unnecessarily restricting the assistant's functionality.

Alternative 3, explainability and user trust in conversational assistants

Investigate how different ways of presenting answers influence users' understanding, trust, and ability to verify information. The work will explore mechanisms such as source citations, links to relevant document sections, supporting evidence, and uncertainty indicators. Different interface designs and explanation strategies will be compared through controlled evaluations or user studies. The objective is to determine how conversational assistants can communicate their answers and limitations transparently, helping students distinguish reliable information from potentially unsupported responses.

Methodology

The project will involve the design and implementation of a server-based conversational assistant that uses a predefined collection of official course documents, including course manuals, assessment guidelines, schedules, learning materials, and frequently asked questions. The assistant will provide a user-friendly web interface and retrieve relevant information from these documents to generate answers.

The student will implement a document processing and retrieval pipeline, integrate an existing language model, and develop the necessary backend and frontend components. The system will serve as an experimental platform for investigating the selected research direction. Developing or fine-tuning a new language model is not required.

Depending on the selected direction, the research may involve comparing retrieval algorithms (e.g., BM25, vector-based search, hybrid retrieval), investigating answer validation and refusal mechanisms, or designing interfaces that present supporting evidence and communicate uncertainty.

A representative evaluation set of course-related questions will be created, including straightforward, ambiguous, complex, and unanswerable questions. Where appropriate, reference answers and supporting document passages will be identified to enable systematic evaluation.

Evaluation methods will depend on the selected research direction and may include retrieval precision and recall, answer correctness, evidence support, hallucination rates, refusal accuracy, response time, and usability or user trust assessments. Experiments will compare alternative system configurations under controlled conditions, with particular emphasis on reproducibility and critical analysis of the results.

The three alternatives are not mutually exclusive. For example, the student may investigate whether improved retrieval reduces hallucinations, or whether presenting source evidence helps users identify incorrect answers. The final scope and combination of research questions will be defined together with the supervisor to ensure a manageable BSc thesis.

Expected outputs

A functional, server-based conversational assistant capable of answering course-related questions using a predefined collection of documents, together with a systematic experimental evaluation of the selected research questions. Expected deliverables include a well-documented software prototype, a reproducible evaluation benchmark, quantitative and/or user-based comparisons of alternative approaches, and an analysis of the trade-offs between answer accuracy, reliability, explainability, and usability. The main research contribution will be the experimental investigation and validation of design choices for trustworthy document-based conversational assistants, rather than the software implementation alone.

BSc thesis · self-supervised learning & signal processing

Are common data augmentations actually valid for physiological signals?

Self-supervised learning uses transformations such as jittering, scaling, cropping and time warping to create training examples. These transformations may also change physiologically meaningful information: time warping an ECG, for example, alters apparent cardiac timing. This thesis will investigate which augmentations preserve the information needed for a selected physiological prediction task.

Objectives & research questions

Objective 1

Measure how augmentation type and strength affect physiological features and downstream prediction.

Objective 2

Test whether a simple feature-preservation criterion helps select useful augmentations rather than assuming that generic time-series transformations are valid.

Methodology

The student will use ECG or PPG from public datasets such as WESAD, with a single classification task and a compact contrastive encoder. Compare three or four augmentations at predefined strengths against supervised and unaugmented baselines.

The student will measure beat timing, heart-rate summaries and waveform characteristics before and after transformation. Separate real physiological variation from transformation-induced changes, accounting for feature-extractor failures.

The student will split participants before creating windows or augmented examples. Tune augmentation choices within training folds and evaluate subject-independent performance and feature distortion across repeated seeds.

Expected outputs

A reproducible augmentation benchmark, a feature-distortion report, downstream performance comparisons and practical recommendations for the selected signal and task. The conclusions will be specific to the tested physiology rather than a universal claim of augmentation validity.

Ideally, an easy-to-use, well-documented open-source library implementing the proposed methods.

References
  • Schmidt, P., Reiss, A., Dürichen, R., Marberger, C., & Van Laerhoven, K. (2018). Introducing WESAD, a Multimodal Dataset for Wearable Stress and Affect Detection. ICMI, 400–408. Paper.
  • Chen, T., Kornblith, S., Norouzi, M., & Hinton, G. (2020). A Simple Framework for Contrastive Learning of Visual Representations. PMLR, 119, 1597–1607. Methodological background from computer vision; physiological validity must be tested separately. Paper.
BSc thesis · affective computing & noisy labels

Detecting potentially incorrect individual emotion labels from physiological data

Emotion annotations can be ambiguous, temporally misaligned or poorly supported by recorded physiology. In a previous work (book chapter accepted for publication) Hortalstrong and Semerci introduce four diagnostics for assessing physiological support for labels at the dataset and annotation-scheme levels. This thesis will extend that work to detecting potentially incorrect labels in individual samples, using controlled label corruption to establish known error locations for evaluation.

Objectives & research questions

Objective 1

Compare out-of-fold disagreement, neighbourhood consistency and ensemble-based scores for recovering deliberately corrupted labels.

Objective 2

Test whether down-weighting or excluding flagged training samples improves classification on untouched held-out subjects.

Methodology

The student will use public datasets such as WESAD or discretised CASE labels; distinguish protocol-assigned stress conditions from verified subjective emotion ground truth. Define windows and label discretisation before experimentation.

The student will introduce known label flips in training data at several rates, including random and temporally clustered corruption. The detector must not access the corruption mask; reserve that mask for evaluation. Treat original annotations as a reference, not certified truth.

The student will generate scores through participant-grouped out-of-fold predictions. Fit neighbours and all preprocessing on the appropriate training fold. Report precision–recall AUC, precision at a fixed review budget and recall of injected errors.

The student will compare removal or weighting against no filtering, random removal and confidence filtering at matched budgets. Tune on training/validation subjects and evaluate once on untouched test subjects. Naturally flagged samples remain candidates for review; without independent annotation, they cannot be declared erroneous.

Expected outputs

A sample-scoring pipeline, a benchmark against known injected errors, an analysis of downstream benefits and a review list of naturally suspicious annotations with documented limitations. Unlike the chapter’s diagnostic battery, the contribution is localising candidate errors and validating that localisation under controlled corruption.

Ideally, an easy-to-use, well-documented open-source library implementing the proposed methods.

BSc thesis · physiological machine learning & uncertainty

Quality-aware prediction reliability, uncertainty and selective prediction

A physiological model can make a confident prediction that is nevertheless incorrect. Previous work by Santamónica, Hortal and colleagues using EEG uses outlier, cohesion, flatness and sharpness measures, alongside recording recency, to select suitable segments. This thesis will investigate whether signal quality provides information about prediction reliability beyond calibrated model confidence, and whether combining the two supports selective prediction.

Objectives & research questions

Objective 1

Compare confidence-only, quality-only and combined reliability estimates.

Objective 2

Evaluate error detection, calibration and the risk–coverage trade-off when a model abstains on predictions estimated to be unreliable.

Methodology

The student will use an existing EEG feature pipeline and accessible I-CARE partitions, subject to data access and computational feasibility. A pre-agreed alternative is one VitalDB task with suitable waveform-quality indicators. Reuse established quality measures rather than proposing another quality score.

The student will keep every patient in the evaluation. For EEG, distinguish usable-EEG and clinical-only cases, mirroring the manuscript’s handling of patients without usable EEG. Avoid assuming that low amplitude or low quality is merely artefact: clinically meaningful abnormal EEG can resemble poor signal quality.

The student will use participant-grouped training, calibration, reliability-model validation and untouched test partitions, or nested grouped cross-validation. Train a lightweight error predictor on held-out or out-of-fold classifier outputs and quality features; never train it on optimistic in-sample errors.

The student will compare calibrated confidence with quality and combined estimates using error-detection AUROC and precision–recall AUC, Brier score or log loss, calibration plots and risk–coverage curves. Select abstention thresholds using validation data. Report coverage and subgroup performance as well as error reduction.

Expected outputs

A quality-aware reliability pipeline, calibrated probability analysis and selective-prediction benchmark. The manuscript reports test AUC 0.868 and Challenge Score 0.694 within 24 hours after return of spontaneous circulation using EEG features and clinical covariates; it does not establish that quality adds error-detection value beyond calibrated confidence. That incremental value, including a possible null result, is the thesis question.

Ideally, an easy-to-use, well-documented open-source library implementing the proposed methods.

BSc thesis · affective computing & domain adaptation

Can we personalise emotion recognition models without emotion labels?

Physiological responses differ between individuals, making personalisation attractive for emotion recognition. However, obtaining labelled emotional recordings from every new user is often impractical. This thesis will investigate whether a short period of unlabelled physiological recording can improve an existing model’s predictions for an unseen participant.

Objectives & research questions

Objective 1

Compare a population model with participant-specific normalisation and one simple distribution-alignment method.

Objective 2

Measure how adaptation benefit changes with the amount of available unlabelled calibration data.

Methodology

The student will use public datasets such as WESAD or CASE and hold out participants. Set aside an initial calibration segment from each target participant and a separate evaluation segment; target evaluation labels remain hidden during adaptation.

The student will compare training-only normalisation, target normalisation and a lightweight alignment method. Any self-supervised adaptation will be kept as an optional extension after the main baselines work.

The student will vary calibration duration and assess balanced accuracy or macro-F1 across participants, including negative transfer and condition imbalance. Do not use future evaluation segments for adaptation unless explicitly presenting a separate transductive setting.

Expected outputs

A reproducible personalisation benchmark, calibration-duration curves and recommendations about when unlabelled adaptation helps or harms. Hortal and Semerci’s XR2Learn chapter motivates careful annotation design, but does not demonstrate successful unlabelled personalisation.

Ideally, an easy-to-use, well-documented open-source library implementing the proposed methods.

BSc thesis · affective computing & representation analysis

Are physiological emotion models learning emotions or recognising individuals?

Physiological features may encode a participant’s identity as well as information associated with affect. A model evaluated using randomly divided recording windows may therefore perform well while generalising poorly to new participants. This thesis will examine identity information in learned representations and its relationship to subject-independent emotion or stress recognition.

Objectives & research questions

Objective 1

Compare emotion or stress performance under random-window and participant-held-out evaluation.

Objective 2

Probe representations for participant identity and test one simple intervention, such as participant normalisation.

Methodology

The student will train a compact model on public datasets such as WESAD or CASE, extract features or intermediate representations, and compare evaluation protocols while preventing overlapping-window leakage.

For identity probing, use separate recording blocks from the same known participants in probe training and testing; an identity classifier cannot meaningfully identify classes absent from its training set. Separately evaluate affect recognition on unseen participants.

The student will control for class balance and recording conditions. Compare raw features, learned features and normalised features; examine whether changes in identity predictability coincide with improved affect transfer. Identity information alone does not prove shortcut use or harm.

Expected outputs

A representation-audit pipeline, a comparison of evaluation protocols and evidence about the trade-off between identity information and affect generalisation. The Hortal and Semerci chapter’s weak physiological support for some labels motivates this audit (see reference below), but is not itself evidence that models learn identity.

Ideally, an easy-to-use, well-documented open-source library implementing the proposed methods.

BSc thesis · medical AI & missing data

Does missing physiological data contain useful predictive information?

Missing physiological measurements may reflect monitoring decisions, clinical interventions or sensor failures. Conventional imputation can overlook useful information in these patterns, while relying on local monitoring practices may weaken generalisation. This thesis will investigate when missingness improves prediction and how robust those improvements are to changes in data availability.

Objectives & research questions

Objective 1

Measure whether missingness flags and time-since-observation improve a fixed baseline.

Objective 2

Test sensitivity to altered missingness patterns and distinguish observed associations from causal interpretations.

Methodology

The student will select one VitalDB task with sufficient naturally missing measurements. Define a prediction cutoff and use only information available before it, avoiding outcome-derived missingness.

The student will compare simple imputation and forward filling with the same models augmented by missingness indicators and elapsed-time features. Fit preprocessing using training patients only.

The student will separate patients across folds and test realistic simulated sensor dropout in addition to natural missingness. Compare discrimination, calibration and subgroup performance at matched feature sets. Avoid inferring why a value is absent unless metadata support that explanation.

Expected outputs

A missing-data benchmark, a robustness analysis and guidance on when missingness features provide useful but potentially fragile information. The Santamónica, Hortal and colleagues' EEG manuscript (see reference below) offers a related design precedent: retaining patients without usable EEG through clinical-only predictions; it does not test the general predictive value of missingness patterns.

Ideally, an easy-to-use, well-documented open-source library implementing the proposed methods.

BSc thesis · medical AI & distribution shift

Can physiological models detect an unfamiliar patient population?

Physiological prediction models may encounter patient groups, procedures or recording conditions that differ from their training data. Detecting such differences could help identify circumstances in which predictions become less reliable. This thesis will compare simple measures of uncertainty and feature distance, examining whether they detect predefined shifts and whether those shifts are associated with poorer predictive performance.

Objectives & research questions

Objective 1

Compare confidence-based and feature-distance-based shift detectors.

Objective 2

Assess whether detecting a shifted group also identifies clinically relevant performance degradation.

Methodology

The student will use one VitalDB prediction task and construct a prespecified shift using sufficiently large procedure categories or patient subgroups. Keep patient identities separate and match or document label-prevalence differences.

The student will fit a baseline classifier and a simple distance detector using source training patients only. Tune thresholds on source validation data; reserve the shifted population for evaluation.

The student will report shifted-group detection AUROC, source false-alarm rates, discrimination and calibration in each group, and error enrichment among flagged cases. A shifted input need not cause an error, and a familiar input can still be misclassified.

Expected outputs

A controlled shift benchmark and an analysis of how closely shift detection tracks model degradation. The EEG manuscript identifies independent-cohort validation as future work; this thesis examines a narrower shift-detection question and will not claim external clinical validation from a single-dataset experiment.

Ideally, an easy-to-use, well-documented open-source library implementing the proposed methods.

BSc thesis · self-supervised learning & physiological machine learning

Can we learn useful physiological representations from very little labelled data?

Self-supervised learning offers a way to use unlabelled physiological recordings when annotated data are scarce. Its benefits depend on the available data, the pretraining approach and the ability to generalise between participants. This thesis will investigate when a compact pretrained model outperforms supervised training for a single physiological prediction task.

Objectives & research questions

Objective 1

Compare supervised training from scratch with self-supervised pretraining at several labelled-participant budgets.

Objective 2

Evaluate cross-participant transfer and the interaction between labelled and unlabelled data availability.

Methodology

The student will choose public datasets such as WESAD and a single stress/affect classification task. Implement a compact contrastive encoder using a small set of justified augmentations.

The student will construct labelled subsets by participant rather than by arbitrary window count. Pretrain only on permitted training participants; exclude held-out test participants for the primary inductive comparison.

The student will use matched architectures, tuning budgets and repeated subset selections. Include a classical feature baseline and report balanced accuracy or macro-F1, uncertainty across participant splits and computational cost.

Expected outputs

Learning curves against labelled-participant and unlabelled-data budgets, a reproducible pretraining pipeline and evidence about when pretraining is worth its cost. Cross-participant transfer and controlled budgets provide the focus beyond a generic supervised-versus-self-supervised comparison.

Ideally, an easy-to-use, well-documented open-source library implementing the proposed methods.

BSc thesis · signal processing & interpretable quality control

Can physiological constraints identify implausible signal segments?

An unusual physiological recording may indicate a meaningful response, a rare condition or a measurement problem. Statistical anomaly detection alone cannot reliably distinguish these possibilities. This thesis will examine whether conservative physiological plausibility constraints improve the detection of corrupted ECG or PPG segments when combined with statistical anomaly scores.

Objectives & research questions

Objective 1

Compare physiological rules, statistical anomaly detection and a simple hybrid detector.

Objective 2

Identify failure modes and false alarms on genuine but unusual physiological segments.

Methodology

The student will use one ECG or PPG dataset, for example WESAD, with realistic injected clipping, dropout, baseline shifts or impulsive artefacts. Use independently annotated real artefacts if available, reporting synthetic and real evaluations separately.

The student will define conservative sensor- and task-specific constraints for timing, continuity and waveform morphology. Record when feature extraction fails rather than silently treating failures as extreme physiological values.

The student will hold out participants and corruption families where feasible. Measure artefact-detection precision–recall, false-positive rates and severity sensitivity. Expert review is required before describing a suspected physiological pattern as truly impossible.

Expected outputs

An interpretable corruption-detection benchmark and documented rules with their limitations. Santamónica and colleagues’ EEG score is related to quality-control background, not a universal plausibility test; this project focuses on ECG/PPG constraint validation rather than reproducing the EEG score.

Ideally, an easy-to-use, well-documented open-source library implementing the proposed methods.

BSc thesis · affective computing & dataset diagnostics

Can simple diagnostics predict the learnability of physiological labels?

Before developing complex physiological models, researchers need evidence that the available signals support the assigned labels. Hortal and Semerci’s previous work (see reference below) uses permutation testing, label flip-rate analysis, Kruskal–Wallis separation and ARI clustering consistency to assess that support. This thesis will extend those diagnostics by testing whether they predict subsequent performance on held-out participants across controlled experimental conditions.

Objectives & research questions

Objective 1

Evaluate the predictive value of diagnostic scores for subsequent subject-independent classification.

Objective 2

Test whether the relationship transfers to unseen corruption levels, label schemes or datasets, rather than simply rerunning the book chapter’s battery.

Methodology

The student will use public datasets such as WESAD and CASE if feasible, creating prespecified label-corruption, granularity and feature-subset conditions. Distinguish temporal label consistency from physiological separability.

The student will compute diagnostics inside training partitions, then measure the performance of fixed downstream classifiers on held-out participants. Use grouped resampling and treat conditions from one dataset as related observations.

The student will fit a simple diagnostic-to-performance model on development conditions and evaluate on held-out conditions or a second dataset. Compare against sample size, class balance and a small supervised baseline; report prediction error and rank correlation.

The student will interpret learnability as conditional on the measured signals, features and model family. The chapter’s findings about timing and granularity are comparative, not causal proof; weak separability does not invalidate an emotion construct or establish incorrect labels.

Expected outputs

A validated diagnostic-to-performance analysis and a lightweight assessment report. The contribution is prospective prediction of downstream performance and its limits, extending the chapter’s dataset-level diagnostics rather than presenting them as a new method.

Ideally, an easy-to-use, well-documented open-source library implementing the proposed methods.

BSc thesis · wearable computing & multimodal signal analysis

Can we distinguish sensor noise from genuine physiological changes?

A sudden change in PPG-derived heart rate may reflect a genuine physiological response or a movement artefact. Signal quality, temporal consistency and agreement between sensors may help distinguish these explanations. This thesis will investigate that distinction using synchronised wearable recordings and an independent cardiac reference where its quality is sufficient.

Objectives & research questions

Objective 1

Compare a single-signal detector with a detector using motion and an independent cardiac reference.

Objective 2

Test whether adding cross-sensor information reduces false alarms on genuine physiological changes.

Methodology

The student will use synchronised WESAD (or any other public dataset) wrist PPG and accelerometry with chest ECG as a reference, subject to alignment and ECG-quality checks. Restrict analysis to segments where the reference can support cardiac timing comparisons.

The student will identify candidate PPG changes, derive reference-consistent and reference-inconsistent cases using transparent criteria, and supplement them with known synthetic artefacts. Motion does not automatically imply noise, and ECG–PPG disagreement is not perfect ground truth.

The student will compare quality-only, temporal-only and combined feature models with participant-held-out evaluation. Report sensitivity to artefacts and specificity for supported real changes, separating natural, ambiguous and synthetic cases.

The student will assess an inference setting using only PPG and accelerometry, reserving ECG for evaluation; any method using ECG at inference must be reported separately.

Expected outputs

A multimodal disambiguation benchmark, a lightweight detector and an error analysis of ambiguous cases. The EEG manuscript provides related quality-indicator ideas, but does not establish that those indicators distinguish sensor artefacts from genuine wearable physiological changes.

Ideally, an easy-to-use, well-documented open-source library implementing the proposed methods.

BSc thesis · multimodal machine learning & sensor selection

When does an additional physiological sensor improve prediction?

An additional physiological sensor may provide complementary information, repeat information already available or introduce further noise. This thesis will estimate the marginal predictive value of selected sensors for a single task, examining how that value changes between participants and under different signal-quality conditions.

Objectives & research questions

Objective 1

Quantify the incremental performance and calibration benefit of each selected sensor.

Objective 2

Test whether sensor value changes under realistic degradation and whether a simple selection policy preserves performance with fewer inputs.

Methodology

The student will use public datasets such as WESAD with two or three modalities, or one VitalDB task if preferred. Define a strong single-sensor baseline and a small set of sensor combinations, avoiding an exhaustive search.

The student will compare models on the same participants, time windows and label definitions, with matched tuning effort. Distinguish adding a sensor from merely increasing model capacity.

The student will estimate paired performance differences with participant-level resampling. Add realistic degradation or dropout and compare feature-level fusion with a simple quality-aware selection rule tuned only on validation data.

The student will report task accuracy, calibration, failure coverage and acquisition burden using documented sensor requirements; avoid inventing monetary costs. Associations and ablation gains are evidence of predictive value rather than causal sensor effects.

Expected outputs

A sensor-ablation study, paired estimates of marginal benefit and a practical selection recommendation. Hortal and Semerci’s XR2Learn chapter motivates checking whether labels are supported before interpreting sensor gains. Santamónica, Hortal and colleagues' EEG manuscript’s EEG-versus-clinical-only validation ablation (see reference below) is a related example of added predictive information, not evidence that any extra wearable sensor will help.

Ideally, an easy-to-use, well-documented open-source library implementing the proposed methods.
